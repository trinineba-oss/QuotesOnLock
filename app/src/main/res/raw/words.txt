# forms: the English words to mark, comma-separated and case-sensitive; "form@a+b" limits one form to those quote types.
# scope: quote type keys (comma-separated) for forms without their own @; empty means anywhere. Written for the app.
Tao,Dao,the Way@laotzu+zhuangzi+confucius+mencius,The Way@laotzu+zhuangzi+confucius+mencius||道|dào|Chinese|the Way|Literally a road or path, and so the way things go: the deep order of the world, which no words can hold. But dào also means “to speak”, and the Tao Te Ching opens with a play on both: 道可道非常道, “the Way that can be spoken is not the constant Way.” Confucius uses the same word for the right way to live.|logos (Greek), ṛta (Sanskrit)
Heaven|laotzu,zhuangzi,confucius,mencius|天|tiān|Chinese|sky, Heaven|Tiān is both the sky overhead and the moral order of the world: not a place where souls go, but the power that gives each thing its nature. When Mencius says that whoever knows his own nature knows Heaven, he means the order within us is the order of the world.|the Tao; kosmos (Greek)
superior man,superior men|confucius,mencius|君子|jūnzǐ|Chinese|the noble person|Originally “a lord's son”, a man of noble birth. Confucius turned it into a rank anyone could earn: a person noble in character, learning and conduct. James Legge's Victorian “superior man” sounds proud; “exemplary person” or “gentleman” comes closer.|kalos kagathos, “the fine and good” (Greek)
virtue,Virtue,virtues|laotzu,zhuangzi,confucius,mencius|德|dé|Chinese|virtue, inner power|Dé is the power a thing has by being fully what it is: the influence a good person has without using force, as the pole star holds the other stars in their places. It is the Te of the Tao Te Ching, “the classic of the Way and its power”.|aretē (Greek), virtus (Latin)
benevolence,Benevolence|confucius,mencius|仁|rén|Chinese|humaneness|The heart of Confucius's teaching. The character joins “person” and “two”: what passes between people at their best. Benevolence, goodness, humaneness and love have all been tried in English, and none quite holds it.|agapē (Greek), karuṇā (Sanskrit)
propriety,Propriety|confucius,mencius|禮|lǐ|Chinese|ritual, propriety|First the rites offered to ancestors and spirits; for Confucius, all the forms of respectful conduct, from ceremony to everyday courtesy, that let people live together gracefully. Practised with a sincere heart, outward form shapes the inner self.|
righteousness|confucius,mencius|義|yì|Chinese|rightness|What is fitting and just in each situation: the duty one owes whatever it costs. Mencius pairs it with rén: benevolence is the heart of a person, righteousness the path it walks.|dikaiosynē (Greek)
sincerity|confucius,mencius|誠|chéng|Chinese|sincerity, integrity|More than honesty: being whole and true all the way through, so that inner and outer are one. The Doctrine of the Mean calls it “the way of Heaven”.|
Self|upanishads,gita|आत्मन्|ātman|Sanskrit|the Self|From a root meaning breath, ātman is the innermost self, beneath body, mind and personality. The great discovery of the Upanishads is that this Self is one with brahman, the reality of the whole universe: tat tvam asi, “thou art that”.|psychē (Greek), anima (Latin)
Brahma,Brahman|upanishads,gita|ब्रह्मन्|brahman|Sanskrit|the Absolute|Not the creator god Brahmā but brahman: the boundless reality underlying all that exists, beyond every name and form. Older English translators, Edwin Arnold among them, often wrote it “Brahma”.|the Tao; the One of Plotinus
Nirvana,nirvana,Nirvāna||निर्वाण|nirvāṇa|Sanskrit|blowing out|Literally the blowing out of a flame: the going out of the fires of greed, hatred and delusion. It is not annihilation but the peace that remains when craving has gone. In Pali, the Buddha's language, it is nibbāna.|ataraxia (Greek)
Dharma,Dhamma,dharma,dhamma||धर्म|dharma|Sanskrit|what holds|From a root meaning “to hold, to support”: the order that upholds the world, the duty that follows from one's place in it, and for Buddhists the Buddha's teaching itself, the truth of how things are.|the Tao; logos (Greek)
Buddha||बुद्ध|buddha|Sanskrit|the awakened one|Not a name but a title, from budh, “to wake up”: one who has woken from the sleep of illusion. Siddhartha Gautama is the Buddha of our age; Buddhists teach that anyone may awaken.|
compassion,Compassion|dhammapada,suttanipata,nhathanh|करुणा|karuṇā|Sanskrit|compassion|The wish that others be free from suffering. It is one of the four “immeasurable” states of heart, with loving-kindness (mettā), joy in others' joy (muditā) and equanimity (upekkhā).|rén (Chinese), agapē (Greek)
mindfulness,Mindfulness||सति|sati|Pali|remembering|Sati first meant memory: keeping something in mind. For the Buddha it is steady, clear awareness of what is happening in body and mind, as it happens.|prosochē, the Stoics' attention (Greek)
suffering,Suffering,pain|dhammapada,suttanipata|दुक्ख|dukkha|Pali|unsatisfactoriness|The subject of the First Noble Truth. Dukkha is pain, but also the dull ache of everything unsatisfying and impermanent; a traditional explanation pictures a wheel that does not run true on its axle. Even pleasure carries dukkha, because it cannot last.|
samsara,Samsara||संसार|saṃsāra|Sanskrit|wandering on|The endless round of birth, death and rebirth, driven by craving and ignorance. Literally “flowing on” or “wandering through”.|
Yoga,yoga||योग|yoga|Sanskrit|yoking|From yuj, “to yoke”, the same root as the English word: a joining, a discipline that harnesses body and mind. In the Gita, yoga is any path that unites a person with the divine: action, knowledge or devotion.|
love|gita|भक्ति|bhakti|Sanskrit|devotion|From bhaj, “to share, to partake”: loving devotion to God, the path of the heart. In the Gita Krishna says that even a leaf, a flower or a little water, offered with love, is accepted.|agapē (Greek)
Veda,Vedas||वेद|veda|Sanskrit|knowledge|Simply “knowledge”, from the same root as Latin videre (to see) and English “wit”: the ancient Sanskrit hymns of India, held to have been heard by the seers rather than composed.|
Guru,guru||गुरु|guru|Sanskrit|weighty one|Literally “heavy”: a teacher whose words carry weight. A traditional reading hears gu (darkness) and ru (remover): the one who drives darkness away.|
Bhikkhu,bhikkhu,Bhikkhus,bhikkhus,Bhikshu,bhikshu||भिक्खु|bhikkhu|Pali|one who lives on alms|A Buddhist monk, who owns almost nothing and depends each day on the generosity of others: a daily lesson in humility and in how we depend on one another.|
Zen||禅|zen|Japanese|meditation|The word travelled with the teaching: Sanskrit dhyāna, meditation, became Chinese chán and Japanese zen. It names a school that points directly at the mind rather than at scriptures.|
Allah||الله|Allāh|Arabic|the God|From al-ilāh, “the God”: the Arabic word for God, used by Arab Christians and Jews as well as by Muslims. It is related to the Hebrew Elohim and the Aramaic Alaha.|
Kismet,kismet||قسمت|qismat|Persian & Turkish, from Arabic|portion, lot|From the Arabic qisma, a share or portion: the lot dealt out to each life. It came into English through Turkish.|Moira (Greek)
dervish,dervishes,Dervish,Dervishes||درویش|darvīsh|Persian|a poor man|A mendicant: a Sufi who has chosen poverty to seek God. Rumi's followers became the Mevlevi order, the whirling dervishes, whose turning dance is a prayer.|Bhikkhu (Pali)
Sufi,Sufis||صوفی|ṣūfī|Arabic|wearer of wool|Probably from ṣūf, wool, after the rough woollen robes of the early ascetics. Sufism is the inward, mystical path of Islam: the way of love toward God.|
Saki,Sákí||ساقی|sāqī|Persian|cupbearer|The one who pours the wine at a gathering. In Persian poetry the Saki is often also the beloved, and to mystical readers the bearer of divine grace.|
wine,Wine|rumi,hafiz,khayyam,attar|شراب|sharāb|Persian|wine|Real wine from the taverns of Shiraz and Nishapur, and also the wine of divine love that makes the mystic “drunk” with God. Persian poets let both meanings shimmer, and readers have argued for centuries over which they meant.|
Beloved,beloved|rumi,hafiz,kabir,mirabai,attar,johncross|معشوق|maʿshūq|Persian & Arabic|the Beloved|In Sufi poetry the Beloved (also called the Friend, yār) is at once a human love and God, the one whose face the soul longs to see. Kabir and Mirabai in India, and John of the Cross in Spain (his Amado), speak the same language of longing.|
vanity,Vanity,vanities,Vanities|ecclesiastes|הֶבֶל|hevel|Hebrew|breath, vapour|Hevel means a breath, a puff of mist on a cold morning: there, then gone. “Vanity of vanities” is the Hebrew way of saying “the most fleeting of all”: not that life is worthless, but that it is as brief and ungraspable as breath.|mujō, impermanence (Japanese)
LORD|psalms,ecclesiastes|יהוה|YHWH|Hebrew|the Name|Small capitals in the King James Bible mark the four letters of God's own name, YHWH, thought too holy to pronounce. Readers said Adonai, “my Lord”, instead, and the translators followed them.|
Word@heraclitus,Logos||λόγος|logos|Greek|word, reason|Logos means word, speech, reason, proportion: the account that makes sense of things. For Heraclitus it is the hidden law by which all things come to pass. Centuries later John's Gospel begins: “In the beginning was the Logos.”|the Tao; dharma (Sanskrit)
virtue,Virtue,virtues|stoic,epictetus,plato,aristotle|ἀρετή|aretē|Greek|excellence|Aretē is excellence of any kind: the sharpness of a knife, the speed of a horse, the goodness of a person. Virtue was not obedience to rules but being excellent at being human. The Latin virtus, from vir (man), first meant courage.|dé (Chinese)
soul,Soul,souls,psyche|plato,aristotle|ψυχή|psychē|Greek|breath, life|Psychē first meant the breath of life that leaves at death. For Socrates and Plato it became the true self, the seat of thought and character, to be cared for above all things. It lives on in “psychology”.|ātman (Sanskrit), anima (Latin)
happiness,Happiness|aristotle|εὐδαιμονία|eudaimonia|Greek|flourishing|Literally “having a good daimon”, a good guardian spirit. For Aristotle it is not a feeling but an activity: a whole life lived well, in accordance with virtue. “Flourishing” comes closer than “happiness”, which is why one swallow does not make a spring.|
wonder|plato|θαυμάζειν|thaumazein|Greek|to wonder|“Wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder” (Theaetetus). A thauma is a marvel, something that makes you stare: philosophy starts when the familiar suddenly seems strange.|
philosophy,Philosophy,philosopher,philosophers||φιλοσοφία|philosophia|Greek|love of wisdom|Philo- (love) and sophia (wisdom): not the possession of wisdom but the love and pursuit of it. Pythagoras is said to have coined it, calling himself a lover of wisdom rather than a wise man.|
leisure|aristotle,stoic|σχολή|scholē|Greek|leisure|Scholē meant free time, and what free people did with it: think, learn, talk. It is the root of the word “school”. For Aristotle leisure, not work, was the purpose of life and the time for philosophy.|otium (Latin)
tranquillity,tranquility|stoic,epictetus|ἀταραξία|ataraxia|Greek|untroubledness|The calm of a mind no longer shaken by fear and desire, the goal of Epicureans, Sceptics and Stoics alike. The Stoics also spoke of apatheia, freedom from the passions, which is not apathy but steadiness.|nirvāṇa (Sanskrit)
Fate,fate,Fates|homer,tragedians|Μοῖρα|Moira|Greek|portion, share|Moira means a share, like a portion at a feast: each life's measured lot. The Moirai, the Fates, spin, measure and cut the thread of every life, and even the gods cannot overturn them.|qismat (Persian)
Muse,Muses||Μοῦσα|Mousa|Greek|Muse|The nine Muses, daughters of Mnemosyne (Memory), inspired poetry, music and learning. Homer begins by asking the Muse to sing through him; “music” and “museum” are their words.|
Eros||Ἔρως|erōs|Greek|desire|Desire: the longing for what one lacks. In Plato's Symposium erōs climbs from love of one beautiful body to the love of beauty itself.|kāma (Sanskrit), ʿishq (Arabic)
agape,charity@psalms,Charity@psalms||ἀγάπη|agapē|Greek|love|The New Testament's word for self-giving love, the love of God and of one's neighbour. The King James Bible of 1611 often rendered it “charity”, from the Latin caritas.|rén (Chinese), karuṇā (Sanskrit)
Fortune,fortune||Fortuna|Fortuna|Latin|luck, chance|The Roman goddess of luck, shown with a wheel that lifts people up and casts them down. Boethius made her wheel a lesson for the Middle Ages: what Fortune gives, she may take back.|Tychē (Greek), qismat (Persian)
Providence||providentia|providentia|Latin|foresight|From providere, “to see ahead”: God's foresight and care guiding all things. The Stoics spoke of pronoia, the providence of the reason that orders the cosmos.|
genius,Genius||genius|genius|Latin|guardian spirit|A Roman's genius was the guardian spirit born with him (from gignere, to beget). Only in the 18th century did “a genius” come to mean a person of extraordinary gifts, rather than a spirit that visits them.|daimōn (Greek)
sublime,Sublime||sublimis|sublimis|Latin|lifted up|Raised aloft. Through Longinus, Burke and Kant it came to mean a greatness that overwhelms us, the storm or the starry sky that mingles awe with something like fear, as against the calm pleasure of the beautiful.|
persona||persona|persona|Latin|mask|The mask worn by actors in the Roman theatre, perhaps from per-sonare, “to sound through”. Jung used it for the face we show the world. Our word “person” comes from it.|
memento mori||memento mori|memento mori|Latin|remember that you must die|The reminder carried in skulls on scholars' desks and, legend says, whispered to Roman generals at their triumphs. Not morbid but bracing: a call to live now.|
amor fati||amor fati|amor fati|Latin|love of fate|Nietzsche's formula for greatness: not merely to bear what is necessary, still less to hide it, but to love it. He wrote it in Latin, though the phrase is his own.|
melancholy,Melancholy||μελαγχολία|melancholia|Greek|black bile|Greek medicine held that too much black bile (melaina cholē) made a person sad, brooding and thoughtful. From the ancients to Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy it was linked with genius as well as gloom.|
ennui,Ennui||ennui|ennui|French|weariness|From the Latin in odio, “in hatred”, through Old French (it shares a root with “annoy”): the gnawing weariness of a soul with nothing worth wanting. Baudelaire called it the worst of all the vices.|acedia (Latin)
longing,Longing|rilke,hesse,goethe,novalis,holderlin,heine,schiller|Sehnsucht|Sehnsucht|German|longing|A deep, aching yearning for something far off and hard to name: home, the past, the infinite. The Romantic poets made it almost holy; C. S. Lewis believed it pointed beyond this world.|saudade (Portuguese)
nostalgia,longing|pessoa|saudade|saudade|Portuguese|longing|Translators of Portuguese often reach for “nostalgia” or “longing” to render saudade: the longing for something absent or lost, perhaps never possessed, sweet and aching at once. There is no single English word for it.|Sehnsucht (German)
anxiety,Anxiety,dread,Dread|kierkegaard|Angest|Angest|Danish|anxiety, dread|Kierkegaard's Angest is not fear of something but the dizziness of freedom: the trembling of a person who sees that he can choose. He called it a teacher. English “dread” and “anxiety” each catch half of it.|Angst (German)
despair,Despair|kierkegaard|Fortvivlelse|fortvivlelse|Danish|despair|For Kierkegaard despair is “the sickness unto death”: not a passing mood but a failure to be oneself, which can afflict even cheerful people. Its cure, he thought, is faith.|
will to power,Will to Power||Wille zur Macht|Wille zur Macht|German|will to power|Nietzsche's name for the basic drive of life: not merely to survive but to grow, overcome and shape. It is often misread as a lust to rule others; its highest form, for Nietzsche, is overcoming oneself.|conatus (Spinoza's Latin)
Superman,superman,Overman,overman||Übermensch|Übermensch|German|over-human|Zarathustra's image of what humanity might become once it creates its own values: the human being who has overcome himself. “Superman” and “overman” have both been tried; neither quite catches über, over and beyond.|
unconscious,Unconscious|freud,jung|das Unbewusste|das Unbewusste|German|the unconscious|Freud's name for the part of the mind we cannot see directly, which shapes our wishes, dreams and slips of the tongue. Jung added a deeper, collective unconscious shared by all humanity, the home of the archetypes.|
ego,Ego|freud|das Ich|das Ich|German|the I|Freud wrote simply das Ich, “the I”, and das Es, “the It”. His English translators turned them into the Latin “ego” and “id”, which made them sound more technical and less intimate than he meant.|
shadow,Shadow|jung|der Schatten|der Schatten|German|the shadow|Jung's name for everything in ourselves we would rather not see. Unacknowledged, the shadow is cast onto other people; owned, it becomes a source of depth and energy.|
individuation|jung|Individuation|Individuation|German|individuation|Jung's word for the lifelong process of becoming whole, the person one truly is, by bringing together the conscious and unconscious sides of the self.|
absurd,the absurd,The absurd|camus|l'absurde|l'absurde|French|the absurd|For Camus the absurd is neither in the world nor in the mind but in the clash between them: our longing for meaning against the world's silence. His answer is not despair but revolt, freedom and passion.|
nothingness,Nothingness|sartre,heidegger|le néant / das Nichts|le néant / das Nichts|French & German|nothingness|The nothing that anxiety reveals. For Heidegger and Sartre it is what lets us step back from what simply is, and so makes freedom possible.|śūnyatā, emptiness (Sanskrit)
Being|heidegger|Sein|das Sein|German|Being|Heidegger asked what it means for anything to be at all: not this or that being but Being itself. We are the beings for whom our own being is a question; he called us Dasein, “being-there”.|
