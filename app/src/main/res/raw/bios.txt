# Short biographies shown on author pages: name|bio (written for the app)
Lao Tzu|Legendary Chinese sage of the 6th century BC (his dates and even existence are uncertain), traditionally the author of the Tao Te Ching, the founding text of Daoism.
Ralph Waldo Emerson|American essayist and poet (1803–1882), leader of Transcendentalism. His essays, such as "Self-Reliance" and "Nature", urged trust in one's own intuition and in the divine in nature.
Benjamin Franklin|American printer, writer, scientist and statesman (1706–1790). His Poor Richard's Almanack and Autobiography made thrift, industry and self-improvement into American proverbs.
Aristotle|Greek philosopher (384–322 BC), pupil of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great. He wrote on nearly everything, from ethics and politics to biology and poetry, and shaped Western thought for two thousand years.
Helen Keller|American author and activist (1880–1968). Deaf and blind from infancy, she learned to read, write and speak with her teacher Anne Sullivan and became a world-famous voice for optimism and social justice.
Eleanor Roosevelt|American diplomat and activist (1884–1962), First Lady from 1933 to 1945, who chaired the drafting of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
Squire Bill Widener|A Virginia countryman whose saying "Do what you can, with what you've got, where you are" Theodore Roosevelt quoted in his Autobiography.
Theodore Roosevelt|26th President of the United States (1858–1919), naturalist, soldier and prolific writer, who preached "the strenuous life" of effort and courage.
Virgil|Roman poet (70–19 BC), author of the Aeneid, the epic of Rome's founding, and of the pastoral Eclogues and Georgics. Dante chose him as his guide through the underworld.
Samuel Johnson|English writer and critic (1709–1784), compiler of the great Dictionary of the English Language, essayist of The Rambler, and subject of Boswell's famous Life.
Voltaire|French writer and philosopher (1694–1778) of the Enlightenment, champion of tolerance and free thought, best known for the satire Candide.
Frederick Douglass|American writer, orator and statesman (c. 1818–1895). He escaped slavery and became the most powerful voice of the abolitionist movement.
Louis Pasteur|French chemist and microbiologist (1822–1895) who showed that germs cause disease and fermentation, and developed vaccines and pasteurisation.
Henry Wadsworth Longfellow|American poet (1807–1882), the most popular poet of his age, author of "A Psalm of Life", Evangeline and The Song of Hiawatha.
Robert Frost|American poet (1874–1963) of rural New England, whose plain-spoken poems such as "The Road Not Taken" and "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" hide deep uncertainty.
Hillel the Elder|Jewish sage and scholar in Jerusalem (c. 110 BC–10 AD), remembered for his gentleness and for summing up the Torah as "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour."
Mark Twain|American writer and humorist (1835–1910), author of Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn, whose wit hid a sharp eye for human folly.
Franklin D. Roosevelt|32nd President of the United States (1882–1945), who led the country through the Great Depression and the Second World War.
James Allen|British writer (1864–1912) whose little book As a Man Thinketh (1903) taught that our thoughts shape our character and our lives.
Samuel Smiles|Scottish writer and reformer (1812–1904) whose Self-Help (1859), full of stories of perseverance, became one of the best-selling books of the Victorian age.
Booker T. Washington|American educator and writer (1856–1915), born into slavery, who founded the Tuskegee Institute and wrote the autobiography Up from Slavery.
William Ernest Henley|English poet and editor (1849–1903). He wrote "Invictus" in hospital after losing a leg to tuberculosis.
Alfred, Lord Tennyson|English poet (1809–1892), Poet Laureate for over forty years, author of In Memoriam, "Ulysses" and "Crossing the Bar".
Socrates|Athenian philosopher (c. 470–399 BC) who wrote nothing but questioned everyone. Sentenced to death for corrupting the young, he is known to us through Plato's dialogues.
Heraclitus|Greek philosopher of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BC), known as "the Obscure", who taught that everything flows and that opposites are bound together. Only fragments of his book survive.
Albert Einstein|German-born physicist (1879–1955) whose theories of relativity changed our understanding of space, time and gravity. He also wrote widely on peace, religion and freedom.
Friedrich Nietzsche|German philosopher (1844–1900) who declared the death of God and asked how we might create new values. His books include Thus Spake Zarathustra and Beyond Good and Evil.
Søren Kierkegaard|Danish philosopher and theologian (1813–1855), often called the father of existentialism. He wrote about anxiety, despair, faith and the single individual.
William Shakespeare|English playwright and poet (1564–1616), author of Hamlet, King Lear, The Tempest and the Sonnets, widely held to be the greatest writer in the English language.
Oscar Wilde|Irish writer and wit (1854–1900), author of The Picture of Dorian Gray and The Importance of Being Earnest. Imprisoned for homosexuality, he wrote De Profundis in his cell.
Isaac Newton|English mathematician and physicist (1643–1727) who discovered the laws of motion and universal gravitation and co-invented calculus.
Henry David Thoreau|American essayist and naturalist (1817–1862) who lived two years in a cabin by Walden Pond and wrote Walden and "Civil Disobedience".
Plato|Athenian philosopher (c. 428–348 BC), student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, whose dialogues, among them the Republic, Symposium and Phaedo, founded Western philosophy.
René Descartes|French philosopher and mathematician (1596–1650), father of modern philosophy, who started from doubt and arrived at "I think, therefore I am".
Protagoras|Greek Sophist (c. 490–420 BC) famous for saying "Man is the measure of all things".
Inscription at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi|The maxims carved at the oracle of Apollo at Delphi, among them "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess", which the Greeks treated as the core of wisdom.
Jean-Jacques Rousseau|Geneva-born philosopher and writer (1712–1778) whose Social Contract, Emile and Confessions shaped modern politics, education and the idea of the self.
Miguel de Cervantes|Spanish novelist (1547–1616), author of Don Quixote, often called the first modern novel.
Francis Bacon|English philosopher and statesman (1561–1626), pioneer of the scientific method and author of the Essays and the Novum Organum.
Arthur Schopenhauer|German philosopher (1788–1860) who saw the world as driven by a blind, restless will, and found consolation in art, compassion and Eastern thought.
Marcus Aurelius|Roman emperor (121–180) and Stoic philosopher. His private notebook, the Meditations, written on campaign, is one of the most loved books of practical wisdom.
Seneca|Roman Stoic philosopher, statesman and dramatist (c. 4 BC–65 AD), tutor to Nero. His Letters to Lucilius are a guide to living well and facing death.
Epictetus|Greek Stoic philosopher (c. 50–135) who was born a slave. His teachings, recorded by his student Arrian, centre on what is and isn't in our power.
Epicurus|Greek philosopher (341–270 BC) who taught that happiness comes from simple pleasures, friendship and freedom from fear, especially the fear of death.
Cicero|Roman statesman, orator and philosopher (106–43 BC) whose essays on friendship, old age and duty brought Greek philosophy to Rome.
The Dhammapada|A collection of 423 verses of the Buddha's teaching in Pali, one of the best-loved texts of early Buddhism.
Emily Dickinson|American poet (1830–1886) who lived quietly in Amherst and wrote nearly 1,800 short, startling poems about death, nature and the inner life, almost none published in her lifetime.
Jane Austen|English novelist (1775–1817), author of Pride and Prejudice and Emma, whose ironic comedies of manners are also studies of judgement and feeling.
Charles Dickens|English novelist (1812–1870), author of Great Expectations, David Copperfield and A Christmas Carol, champion of the poor and the greatest storyteller of his age.
Aesop|Greek storyteller (c. 620–564 BC), said to have been a slave, to whom hundreds of animal fables with morals are attributed.
George Eliot|Pen name of Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), English novelist of Middlemarch and The Mill on the Floss, famed for her moral depth and sympathy.
Walt Whitman|American poet (1819–1892) whose Leaves of Grass, in free verse, celebrated the self, the body, democracy and the common people.
Kahlil Gibran|Lebanese-American poet, writer and painter (1883–1931) whose book The Prophet, a series of poetic sermons on love, work, joy and death, has been read by millions.
Rainer Maria Rilke|Bohemian-Austrian poet (1875–1926) of the Duino Elegies and Sonnets to Orpheus, whose Letters to a Young Poet are a guide to solitude and inner life.
Fyodor Dostoevsky|Russian novelist (1821–1881) of Crime and Punishment and The Brothers Karamazov, who explored faith, freedom, guilt and suffering.
Leo Tolstoy|Russian novelist (1828–1910) of War and Peace and Anna Karenina, who later turned to a radical Christian ethic of nonviolence and simplicity.
Franz Kafka|Prague-born German-language writer (1883–1924) of The Trial and The Metamorphosis, whose stories of anxiety and absurd authority gave us the word "Kafkaesque".
Albert Camus|French-Algerian writer and philosopher (1913–1960) of The Stranger, The Plague and The Myth of Sisyphus, who answered the absurd with revolt and solidarity. Nobel Prize 1957.
Hermann Hesse|German-Swiss novelist and poet (1877–1962) of Siddhartha, Steppenwolf and The Glass Bead Game, about the search for the self. Nobel Prize 1946.
Confucius|Chinese teacher and philosopher (551–479 BC) whose sayings, collected in the Analects, made virtue, learning, ritual and family duty the foundation of Chinese culture.
Rumi|Persian poet and Sufi mystic (1207–1273). His Masnavi and lyric poems of longing for the divine Beloved are among the most widely read poetry in the world.
Omar Khayyam|Persian mathematician, astronomer and poet (1048–1131). The quatrains attributed to him, made famous in English by Edward FitzGerald, celebrate the fleeting moment.
Sun Tzu|Chinese general and strategist (traditionally 6th century BC), author of The Art of War.

Giacomo Leopardi|Italian poet and philosopher (1798–1837) of the Canti and the Zibaldone, whose lucid pessimism is matched by the beauty of his verse.
