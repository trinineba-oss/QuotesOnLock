# Reading paths: P|title|subtitle|tint (hex) then S|q or i|exact quote text (\n for line breaks) or idea title|step label|why this step (written for the app)
P|An introduction to existentialism|From Kierkegaard's dizziness to Camus's happy Sisyphus|7A5ADC
S|i|Life Understood Backwards|Where it begins|Existentialism starts with a plain observation from Kierkegaard: we have to live our lives before we can understand them. No theory hands us a finished meaning in advance; we choose on the way.
S|i|The Dizziness of Freedom|Anxiety|Once we see that we are free, we feel it in the body as a kind of vertigo. Kierkegaard thought this anxiety was not a disorder but a sign of our freedom, the first thing to notice on this path.
S|i|The Knight of Faith|A leap|Kierkegaard's answer to the dizziness was a leap of faith, lived out in ordinary joy. Not every existentialist followed him, but all of them kept his question: how do I become myself?
S|i|God Is Dead|The ground gives way|Nietzsche named the crisis behind the movement: if the old certainties no longer hold, where do values come from? He hoped we would learn to create them rather than despair.
S|q|Man is condemned to be free. Condemned because he did not create himself, yet is nevertheless at liberty, and from the moment he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does.|Condemned to be free|Sartre takes Nietzsche's challenge to its end: with no fixed human nature, we are responsible for what we make of ourselves. It sounds heavy, and it is also a kind of dignity.
S|i|Bad Faith|Hiding from freedom|If freedom is so heavy, we look for ways to escape it: "I had no choice", "that's just who I am". Sartre called this bad faith. Watch for it in yourself, gently.
S|i|One Is Not Born a Woman|Freedom in a situation|Beauvoir showed that freedom is always lived inside a situation: a body, a society, other people's expectations. Becoming free means seeing how we were shaped, and choosing anew.
S|q|The struggle itself towards the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy.|The happy Sisyphus|The path ends with Camus. The world will not answer our longing for meaning, yet the effort itself, lived fully and lucidly, can fill a heart. Where does your own boulder lie, and can you smile at it?
P|The Stoics in a week|Seven days with Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus|3C78C8
S|i|What Is in Our Power|Day 1 · The one distinction|Everything in Stoicism follows from Epictetus's first lesson: some things are up to us, our judgements, aims and actions, and most things are not. Today, sort one worry into the right pile.
S|q|Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. Thus death is nothing terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death, that it is terrible.|Day 2 · Between event and feeling|It is not the event that upsets us but the story we tell about it. Catch one story today and ask whether it is the only true one.
S|q|We suffer more often in imagination than in reality.|Day 3 · Fear at its true size|Seneca wrote this to a friend anxious about the future. Most of what we dread never happens; what does is rarely as we pictured. The Solace section has a practice built on this line.
S|q|It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it.|Day 4 · Time|Seneca's great theme. Notice where today's hours go, and give one of them to something you value.
S|q|Say this to yourself in the morning: Today I shall have to do with meddlers, with the ungrateful, with the insolent, with the crafty, with the envious and the selfish. All these vices have beset them, because they know not what is good and what is evil.|Day 5 · Preparing for people|Marcus prepared each morning to meet difficult people, not to judge them, but so they would not knock him off balance. He reminds himself that we are made to work together.
S|i|The View from Above|Day 6 · Seeing it small|Marcus would picture the whole earth from high above: cities as anthills, our troubles as specks. It is not meant to make us feel small, but free.
S|i|Amor Fati|Day 7 · Loving what happens|The week ends with acceptance, not resignation: to want what actually happens, and make good use of it. Nietzsche took the phrase; the attitude is pure Stoic. Look back over the week: what did you learn to accept?
P|Myths of love and loss|Orpheus, Psyche, the Weaver Girl and more|C86EA0
S|i|Orpheus Looks Back|Love that cannot let go|Orpheus sang his way into the underworld for Eurydice, and lost her by turning to look. The oldest stories of love already know that holding too tightly can lose what we hold.
S|i|Psyche's Doubt|Love and trust|Psyche could not bear to love without seeing. Her journey back to Eros is long and hard, and ends in a marriage of soul and love. Trust, the myth says, is earned through trials.
S|i|Isis Seeks Osiris|Love that searches|Isis searched the whole of Egypt for her husband's scattered body and made him whole again. Grief here is active: an act of devotion that restores.
S|i|Penelope's Web|Love that waits|Penelope wove by day and unwove by night for twenty years, keeping faith while the world assumed her husband dead. Waiting, too, can be a form of courage.
S|i|Baucis and Philemon|Love that grows old|An old couple shared their poor meal with disguised gods and asked only to never be parted. They became an oak and a linden, growing side by side.
S|i|The Weaver Girl and the Cowherd|Love across distance|Separated by the river of stars, the lovers meet once a year on a bridge of magpies. A myth for anyone who loves someone far away.
S|i|Ariadne on Naxos|Love after abandonment|Ariadne woke alone on an island, betrayed. Yet there a new love found her. Loss, in the myths, is rarely the end of the story.
S|i|Savitri and Yama|Love stronger than fear|Savitri followed Death himself and won her husband back with wit and devotion. The path closes with love that refuses to give up. Which of these stories feels most like yours?
P|How to be alone|Solitude as a gift, not a sentence|2E8B74
S|q|I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber. A man who has enough to live on, if he knew how to stay with pleasure at home, would not leave it to go to sea or to besiege a town.|Why we flee|Pascal saw that we fill every moment with distraction because we cannot bear to sit still with ourselves. The path begins by noticing that flight.
S|q|Thinking, existentially speaking, is a solitary but not a lonely business; solitude is that human situation in which I keep myself company. Loneliness comes about when I am alone without being able to split up into the two-in-one, without being able to keep myself company.|Solitude is not loneliness|Arendt drew the line that matters: loneliness is being cut off; solitude is keeping yourself company. The rest of this path is about learning the second.
S|q|Solitude is a silent storm that breaks down all our dead branches; Yet it sends our living roots deeper into the living heart of the living earth.|What solitude does|Gibran pictures solitude as a storm that strips away the dead branches and drives our roots deeper. It can be uncomfortable, and it is how we grow.
S|q|Solitude is the place of purification.|A clearing|Buber, the philosopher of encounter, still valued time alone: a place to return to before we meet others again more truly.
S|q|Sing and dance together and be joyous, but let each one of you be alone, even as the strings of a lute are alone though they quiver with the same music.|Alone together|Gibran's advice on marriage: closeness needs space. Like the strings of a lute, we are separate and play the same music.
S|i|Living Deliberately|An experiment|Thoreau went to Walden to see what life needs when we strip it back. You don't need a cabin: an hour alone, unhurried, is the same experiment.
S|i|Sitting Quietly in a Room|Practice|The path ends where it began, with Pascal's room, but now as a practice. Sit for five minutes with nothing to do. What do you find there?
P|The examined life|From Socrates to Thoreau, learning to know yourself|D2A050
S|i|Know Thyself|The inscription|Carved at Delphi, the oldest advice in Western philosophy: before anything else, understand who you are, and what you are not.
S|i|Socratic Wisdom|Knowing that we don't know|Socrates found he was wiser than others only because he knew the limits of his knowledge. Examination begins with that humility.
S|i|The Examined Life|Why it matters|At his trial Socrates said an unexamined life is not worth living. He meant a life lived only by habit, never chosen.
S|i|Eudaimonia|What we are aiming at|Aristotle asked what all our choices are for, and answered: a flourishing life, lived well over its whole length. Examination serves that.
S|i|What Do I Know?|The essay as a mirror|Montaigne sat in his tower and wrote about himself, honestly and with humour, inventing the essay. His motto was a question: what do I know?
S|i|Philosophy as a Way of Life|Daily practice|For the ancients, philosophy was not a subject but a way of living, with daily exercises of attention. Pierre Hadot recovered this idea in our time.
S|i|Living Deliberately|Living it|Thoreau's experiment at Walden was examination in action: strip life back and see what it really needs. What would you strip back, if you could?
P|Wonder: the universe and us|From deep time to the pale blue dot|3C5AA0
S|i|The Silence of Infinite Spaces|The vertigo|Pascal looked at the night sky and felt dread at its silence. Begin there: the universe is vast, and does not explain itself.
S|i|Deep Time|How old|Geologists discovered that the earth is unimaginably old: billions of years, not thousands. Our whole history is a moment.
S|i|The Cosmic Calendar|A year of everything|Squeeze the universe into one year and all of human history happens in the last seconds before midnight on 31 December.
S|i|Endless Forms Most Beautiful|Life unfolding|Darwin ended his great book with wonder: from simple beginnings, endless forms most beautiful have been, and are being, evolved.
S|i|We Are Made of Star-Stuff|Where we come from|The atoms in our bodies were forged in stars. We are the universe become aware of itself.
S|i|The Pale Blue Dot|How small|Seen from Voyager, the earth is a speck in a sunbeam. Everyone we have ever loved lived there.
S|i|The Overview Effect|How precious|Astronauts who see the earth from space often come back changed: borders vanish, care grows. Wonder turns into tenderness.
S|i|Awe|Living with wonder|Psychologists find that awe makes us feel smaller and more connected, kinder and less anxious. Step outside tonight and look up.
P|The way of the East|The Tao, the Buddha and Zen|B4643C
S|i|The Tao That Can Be Told|The nameless|The Tao Te Ching opens by saying the true Way cannot be put into words. Hold your concepts lightly on this path.
S|i|The Way of Water|Softness|Water is soft and yielding, yet wears away stone. Laozi saw in it the model for a good life: humble, patient, nourishing.
S|i|Doing by Not-Doing|Wu wei|Act without forcing; let things take their course. Not laziness, but the effortless skill of someone in harmony with what is.
S|i|The Four Noble Truths|The Buddha's diagnosis|Life involves suffering; craving drives it; it can end; there is a path. The Buddha spoke like a doctor.
S|i|The Middle Way|Neither extreme|The Buddha tried luxury and then harsh austerity, and found awakening between them. Balance is the path.
S|i|The Second Arrow|Not adding to pain|The first arrow is pain; the second is our reaction to it. We can learn not to shoot the second.
S|i|Beginner's Mind|Fresh eyes|In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's, few. Zen asks us to meet each moment new.
S|i|Ichi-go Ichi-e|This meeting, once|Each encounter happens only once. The tea ceremony's lesson closes the path: be fully present to whatever is in front of you now.
P|Through hard times|Voices for when life is heavy|5A7A50
S|q|In the depth of winter, I finally learned that within me there lay an invincible summer.|Something that holds|Camus found, in a cold season, something in himself that winter could not reach. The path begins with that hope: there is more in you than this moment.
S|q|Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way.|The last freedom|Frankl, in the camps, saw that even with everything gone we keep the freedom to choose our attitude. It is small, and it is enough to stand on.
S|i|Meaning Makes Suffering Bearable|For what|Frankl's discovery: people endure almost anything if they have a reason, a person, a task, a love. What is yours today?
S|q|Your pain is the breaking of the shell that encloses your understanding. Even as the stone of the fruit must break, that its heart may stand in the sun, so must you know pain.|Pain that opens|Gibran doesn't call pain good, only that it can open us. That opening is something to notice when it comes.
S|i|The Second Arrow|Not adding to it|Pain is hard enough; we often add self-blame and fear on top. The Buddha's teaching helps us drop that second arrow.
S|i|Kintsugi|Mended with gold|Japanese potters repair broken bowls with gold, making the break part of the beauty. We are not less for having been broken.
S|i|Post-Traumatic Growth|Growing through it|Many people find, after great difficulty, deeper relationships and a clearer sense of what matters. Not because suffering is good, but because we can grow.
S|q|Hope is the thing with feathers\nThat perches in the soul,\nAnd sings the tune without the words,\nAnd never stops at all,\nAnd sweetest in the gale is heard;\nAnd sore must be the storm\nThat could abash the little bird\nThat kept so many warm.\nI 've heard it in the chillest land,\nAnd on the strangest sea;\nYet, never, in extremity,\nIt asked a crumb of me.|Hope|The path closes with Dickinson's bird, singing in the gale and asking nothing. If things feel too heavy, the Solace section and "When it's hard" are there too.
P|Love in many forms|From Plato's ladder to Tennyson's grief|C8506E
S|i|The Ladder of Love|Where love begins|Plato starts with ordinary attraction to one person and lets it widen, rung by rung, to love of all that is beautiful and good. Love here is a teacher, leading us out of ourselves.
S|q|Let me not to the marriage of true minds\nAdmit impediments, love is not love\nWhich alters when it alteration finds,\nOr bends with the remover to remove.\nO no, it is an ever-fixed mark\nThat looks on tempests and is never shaken;\nIt is the star to every wand'ring bark,\nWhose worth's unknown, although his height be taken.\nLove's not Time's fool, though rosy lips and cheeks\nWithin his bending sickle's compass come,\nLove alters not with his brief hours and weeks,\nBut bears it out even to the edge of doom:\nIf this be error and upon me proved,\nI never writ, nor no man ever loved.|Love that stays|Shakespeare's sonnet sets love against time and change: real love is "an ever-fixed mark" that doesn't bend when the other person changes or the years pass.
S|q|Love gives naught but itself and takes naught but from itself. Love possesses not nor would it be possessed; for love is sufficient unto love.|Love that does not possess|Gibran's Prophet adds a freedom to that steadiness. Love is complete in itself; it doesn't need to own, or be owned.
S|q|Love one another, but make not a bond of love: let it rather be a moving sea between the shores of your souls.|Space in togetherness|The same book, on marriage: stand together, yet not too near, like the pillars of a temple. Closeness that leaves room for each person to grow.
S|q|‘Love seeketh not itself to please,\nNor for itself hath any care,\nBut for another gives its ease,\nAnd builds a heaven in hell’s despair.’|Love that gives|Blake's clod of clay sings of love that builds a heaven in hell's despair, by caring for another before itself. (In the poem a pebble answers with love's selfish side; Blake lets both speak.)
S|i|Layla and Majnun|Love as longing|Persian poets told of Majnun, so consumed by love for Layla that he became a byword for madness. Sufi readers heard in his longing the soul's longing for God.
S|q|Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame. Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned.|Strong as death|The Song of Songs, the Bible's love poem, claims love is as strong as death and that many waters cannot quench it.
S|q|I hold it true, whate'er befall;\nI feel it, when I sorrow most;\n'Tis better to have loved and lost\nThan never to have loved at all.|When love is lost|Tennyson wrote In Memoriam over many years after his closest friend died young. Grief, he found, was the price of love, and still worth paying.
P|Nature as teacher|What woods, ponds and flowers have taught|4E8A4A
S|q|I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived.|Living deliberately|Thoreau went to Walden Pond to strip life to its essentials and see what nature had to teach. This path follows the same question: what does the natural world teach us?
S|q|The world is too much with us; late and soon,\nGetting and spending, we lay waste our powers:\nLittle we see in nature that is ours;\nWe have given our hearts away, a sordid boon!|What we forget|Wordsworth, two centuries ago, already felt that getting and spending had cut people off from the natural world. He would rather be a pagan who could still feel it.
S|q|To see a World in a grain of sand,\nAnd a Heaven in a wild flower,\nHold Infinity in the palm of your hand,\nAnd Eternity in an hour.|Seeing closely|Blake turns the lens around: look closely enough at the smallest thing, a grain of sand, a wild flower, and the infinite opens.
S|q|The old pond!\nA frog leapt into —\nList, the water sound!|One moment|Bashō's most famous haiku: an old pond, a frog, the sound of water. Nature teaches attention to a single moment, fully heard.
S|q|When we try to pick out anything by itself, we find it hitched to everything else in the universe.|Everything hitched|John Muir, wandering the Sierra, saw what ecologists would later map: nothing in nature stands alone. Pull one thread and the whole web moves.
S|i|The Three Sisters|Growing together|Corn, beans and squash, planted together by many Indigenous peoples, each help the others thrive. A garden can be a lesson in cooperation.
S|q|Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin: And yet I say unto you, That even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these.|Without anxiety|In the Sermon on the Mount, the lilies teach freedom from worry: they neither toil nor spin, yet are dressed more finely than Solomon.
S|i|Silent Spring|Caring for it|Rachel Carson showed how poisons travel through the web Muir described, and turned wonder into responsibility. To learn from nature is also to look after it.
S|q|Heaven is under our feet as well as over our heads.|Under our feet|Thoreau again, to close: we needn't look up or away for wonder. It is in the ground we stand on.
P|Courage|What the wise have said about being brave|B0703A
S|q|Courage is resistance to fear, mastery of fear, not absence of fear.|What courage is|Mark Twain, who knew plenty of fear, puts it plainly: courage isn't the absence of fear but the mastery of it. This path walks through what the wise have said about being brave.
S|i|The Choice of Heracles|The hard road|The Greek parable of the crossroads: ease on one side, effort and honour on the other. Courage often begins with choosing the harder path.
S|q|It is the part of grovellers and cowards to follow the safe track; courage loves a lofty path.|The lofty path|Seneca, who lived dangerously close to Nero, says the same in a sentence: courage loves a lofty path.
S|q|And if it is a fear you would dispel, the seat of that fear is in your heart and not in the hand of the feared.|Where fear lives|Gibran turns inward: the seat of a fear is in our own heart, not in the thing we fear. That is where courage does its work.
S|q|Our doubts are traitors,\nAnd make us lose the good we oft might win\nBy fearing to attempt.|Daring to try|Shakespeare's Lucio urges Isabella to go and plead for her brother. Doubt, he says, makes us lose what we might win, simply by fearing to attempt it.
S|i|The Courage to Be|Courage in the face of anxiety|The theologian Paul Tillich widened courage beyond the battlefield: affirming your life in spite of anxiety about death, meaninglessness and guilt.
S|q|Remember, therefore, for the future, upon all occasions of sorrow, to use the maxim: this thing is not misfortune, but to bear it bravely is good fortune.|Bearing it bravely|Marcus Aurelius gives a maxim for hard days: this is not misfortune, but to bear it bravely is good fortune.
S|i|Gawain and the Green Knight|Courage and honesty|Gawain keeps his word and faces the axe, yet keeps the green girdle to save himself. The poem honours him all the same: courage includes owning our failures.
P|Stories of the Caribbean|Anansi, Papa Bois, Mama Dlo and the spirits of the night|2F8F9D
S|i|How Anansi Got the Stories|Where the stories come from|Caribbean storytelling begins in West Africa, with Anansi the spider, who won all the world's stories from the sky god by his wits. Enslaved Akan people carried him across the Atlantic.
S|i|Jack Mantora|How a story ends|In Jamaica every Anansi story ended with the same words, a gentle way of telling the listeners that the tale's punishments weren't meant for them.
S|i|Papa Bois|The keeper of the forest|Trinidad and Tobago's forest guardian protects the animals from greedy hunters. Greet him politely, and never stare at his feet.
S|i|Mama Dlo|The mother of the waters|Half woman, half great snake, she guards the rivers and punishes those who foul them. Papa Bois and Mama Dlo together keep forest and river.
S|i|La Diablesse|A stranger on the road|The beautiful woman in the wide hat, with one cow's hoof beneath her skirt: a warning against following strangers into the night.
S|i|The Soucouyant|The fireball in the night|The old woman who sheds her skin and flies as a ball of fire. The defences are delightfully practical: salt for her skin, rice for her to count.
S|i|The Douens|Feet turned backwards|Spirits of unbaptised children in big straw hats, whose backward feet lead followers astray. The stories kept children near home at dusk.
S|i|Gang Gang Sara|A longing for home|Tobago's legend of the African woman who flew in on the wind and, having eaten salt, could not fly home again: a story holding a whole people's memory of Africa.
S|i|The Moko Jumbie|The guardian on stilts|The path ends in Carnival, where the moko jumbie still dances high over the crowd, a living folklore that children learn today.
P|Through creative block|Seven days with makers who got stuck, and got going again|B0703A
S|q|Art is not about thinking something up. It is the opposite — getting something down.|Day 1 · Getting something down|Julia Cameron's reminder that making is not waiting for ideas but putting things down. Today, put down anything at all: a line, a sketch, a few notes. Quality is not the point yet.
S|q|If you hear a voice within you saying: "you are no painter," then paint by all means, lad, and that voice will be silenced, but only by working.|Day 2 · The voice that says you can't|Van Gogh, who began late and was told he had no gift, knew the critic well. You can't argue it away; you can work while it talks. Make ten minutes of deliberately bad work today.
S|i|Morning Pages|Day 3 · Three pages|Try Cameron's morning pages for one morning: three pages of longhand, first thing, about anything. The Solace section has a practice to start you off.
S|i|Filling the Well|Day 4 · Filling the well|If you feel empty, put something in before you try to take something out. Take yourself somewhere that delights you, alone, for an hour: Cameron's artist date.
S|i|Negative Capability|Day 5 · Staying in the dark|Keats thought great writers could stay in uncertainty without reaching irritably after answers. Not knowing what comes next is part of the work, not a failure of it.
S|q|This is how you do it: you sit down at the keyboard and you put one word after another until its done. It's that easy, and that hard.|Day 6 · One word after another|Neil Gaiman's whole method, and it works for any art. Set a time tomorrow, sit down, and do the next small piece.
S|q|I am I: and I must follow that furrow, not copy another. That is the only justification for my writing, living.|Day 7 · Your own furrow|Virginia Woolf's vow in her diary: not to be famous or great, but to follow her own way. The best cure for block may be to stop trying to make someone else's work, and make your own.
