# Where to start reading each major author (written for the app): name|suggestion
Kahlil Gibran|Start with The Prophet (1923): twenty-six short sermons you can read one at a time, and the whole of it is in the Library. Then Sand and Foam, a book of aphorisms, and The Madman, his strange early parables.
Vincent van Gogh|Start with his letters to his brother Theo, in the Library, read a few at a time from the miners' villages to Arles. The essay A great fire in the soul is a short way in.
Friedrich Nietzsche|Start with The Gay Science (also translated The Joyful Wisdom), the most playful of his books, then Beyond Good and Evil. Thus Spake Zarathustra is his most famous, but best read once you know his voice.
Rabindranath Tagore|Start with Gitanjali, in the Library: 103 short song offerings. Then Stray Birds, a book of tiny poems, and Sadhana, his lectures on the meaning of life.
Seneca|Start with his Letters to Lucilius: short, warm, practical letters on time, fear, friendship and death. Then the essay On the Shortness of Life.
Ralph Waldo Emerson|Start with the essay Self-Reliance, then Nature and The Over-Soul. His essays are best read aloud, a few paragraphs at a time.
Marcus Aurelius|Start with the Meditations, in the Library: open Book II for the famous morning rehearsal, then read anywhere, a few entries at a time.
Rumi|Start with a short selection of his lyric poems; the long Masnavi is best dipped into later. Read slowly, a poem a day.
Franz Kafka|Start with the short stories, The Metamorphosis and Before the Law, then the aphorisms he wrote at Zürau. The Trial is the novel to read next.
Arthur Schopenhauer|Start with his Essays and Aphorisms, which are clear, witty and much easier than his great book, The World as Will and Idea.
Henry David Thoreau|Start with Walden, especially the chapters Where I Lived, and What I Lived For and Solitude. Then the short essay Walking.
Michel de Montaigne|Start with the short essays: Of Idleness, Of Friendship, and That to Philosophise Is to Learn to Die. Then wander; he invented the essay as a ramble.
Leo Tolstoy|Start with The Death of Ivan Ilyich, a short novel that holds everything he cared about. Then Anna Karenina, and War and Peace when you have the time.
Blaise Pascal|Start with the Pensées, opened anywhere: they are fragments, some a single line. Look for the thinking reed and the man who cannot sit quietly in a room.
Søren Kierkegaard|Start with the Journals, where his voice is most personal, then Fear and Trembling, a short book about Abraham and the leap of faith.
Fyodor Dostoevsky|Start with Notes from Underground (short and fierce), then Crime and Punishment. The Brothers Karamazov is his summit.
Epictetus|Start with the Enchiridion, the little handbook of his teaching: you can read it in an hour and return to it for years.
Confucius|Start with the Analects, the short sayings collected by his students; read a few each day and let them accumulate.
Oscar Wilde|Start with The Importance of Being Earnest, then the fairy tales such as The Happy Prince, and finally De Profundis, the long letter he wrote in prison.
George Eliot|Start with Middlemarch: long, but generous and wise. Silas Marner is a short, warm way in first.
Carl Jung|Start with Memories, Dreams, Reflections, his late autobiography, then Modern Man in Search of a Soul.
Albert Camus|Start with The Stranger, a short novel, then the essay The Myth of Sisyphus. The Plague shows his ethics in action.
Goethe|Start with The Sorrows of Young Werther, the short novel that made him famous at twenty-four, then Faust, Part One.
Rainer Maria Rilke|Start with Letters to a Young Poet, ten letters of advice that read as advice for anyone. Then the poems of The Book of Hours.
William James|Start with the essays The Will to Believe and Is Life Worth Living?, then The Varieties of Religious Experience.
Giacomo Leopardi|Start with the short poem L'infinito (The Infinite), then his Essays and Dialogues, where bleakness and wit meet.
Plato|Start with the death of Socrates: the Apology, Crito and the last scene of the Phaedo, all in the Library with guides. Then the Symposium.
Edgar Allan Poe|Start with The Raven and the short stories The Tell-Tale Heart and The Fall of the House of Usher.
Ivan Turgenev|Start with A Sportsman's Sketches, quiet scenes of Russian country life, then the novel Fathers and Sons (also translated Fathers and Children).
Joseph Conrad|Start with Heart of Darkness, a short novel, then Lord Jim.
Hermann Hesse|Start with Siddhartha, short and luminous, then Demian and Steppenwolf.
Herman Melville|Start with Bartleby, the Scrivener, a short story, before setting out on Moby-Dick.
William Blake|Start with Songs of Innocence and of Experience, short poems in pairs, then the Proverbs of Hell in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell.
Walt Whitman|Start with Song of Myself from Leaves of Grass, read aloud.
William Shakespeare|Start with a performance rather than the page: Twelfth Night or A Midsummer Night's Dream, then Hamlet and King Lear. The sonnets reward slow reading.
Anton Chekhov|Start with the short stories The Lady with the Dog and The Student, then the play Uncle Vanya.
Lao Tzu|Start with the Tao Te Ching, in the Library: 81 short chapters, with a guide. Read one a day.
Baruch Spinoza|Start with a good introduction before the Ethics itself, which is written like geometry; then read Part Five, on freedom.
David Hume|Start with the short autobiography My Own Life, then An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding.
Charles Baudelaire|Start with the prose poems in Paris Spleen, then The Flowers of Evil.
