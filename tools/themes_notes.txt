Time|Time begins as a river no one can step into twice. Augustine finds it a riddle he lives in but cannot explain; the mystics set it against eternity. Bergson makes it the stuff of conscious life and Borges the stuff of the self. Physics then calls it perhaps an illusion, while Kawabata reminds us that each of us flows through it differently.
The self|The Upanishads find the self one with everything ("thou art it"); Mencius and Augustine look inward and find Heaven or God. Hume looks in and finds only passing perceptions, Kierkegaard a self that must choose itself, Buber a self made in meeting others. By Pessoa the self is the gap between selves, and Achebe insists no one else can tell you who you are.
Suffering|The Greeks say we learn by suffering; the Stoics that the mind need not be harmed by it. The mystics find the remedy hidden where the pain is, and Johnson prescribes activity. Dostoevsky and Unamuno make suffering the proof that we love and exist, Frankl finds meaning in it, and Thich Nhat Hanh turns to the suffering of the one who hurts us.
Love|Love is first a force strong enough to bind the gods, then Plato's longing for wholeness and the mystics' road to God. Spinoza defines it coolly as joy with a cause; Hugo sees it outlive a ruined heart. The 20th century makes love something we do and see: Buber's meeting, Fromm's activity, Murdoch's discovery that someone else is real, Baldwin's growing up.
Freedom|For Epictetus freedom is inward; for Rumi it is loving what holds you; for Dante it is God's greatest gift. Cervantes and Milton praise freedom of body and of speech, and Thoreau asks what political freedom is for. Sartre finds us condemned to it, Mandela makes it a duty to others' freedom, and Le Guin calls it a burden we choose.
God|The Upanishads find God hidden in the heart; the medieval mystics find God in the soul's depths and in the order of all things, and Browne calls nature God's art. In the modern age the search grows restless: Kierkegaard's faith, Tolstoy's God-as-life, van Gogh's nameless something, Kazantzakis's God as the search itself, and Weil's God present as absence.
Death|Zhuangzi wonders whether death may be life; the Stoics call it nature's law, nothing to dread. Han Shan compares it to ice melting into water, and Montaigne asks why we fear what we do not know. Hazlitt reminds us there was a time when we were not. Tagore and Murakami make death part of life, and Forster finds that the idea of death saves us.
Happiness|The Buddha and the Stoics place happiness in the mind and in virtue; Augustine longs for a happiness that can say "it is enough". Schopenhauer doubts it is more than relief from pain. The moderns find it sideways: Russell in the full use of our faculties, Krishnamurti when we stop seeking it, Camus in harmony with the life we lead, Merton in choosing the one thing needed.
Truth|Truth is first what is heard behind appearances, and the Stoic seeks it gladly, ready to be corrected. Boethius, Eckhart and Ryōkan look for it within; Chaucer calls it the highest thing a man may keep, while Bacon's Pilate will not wait for an answer. Emerson ties it to justice, Unamuno suspects we seek life in it, and Camus warns that it is not what we would like.
Nature|Homer sees us as leaves; Marcus as parts cut off from the whole; the Chinese poets find quiet among mountains and seasons. Emerson and Ruskin make nature a teacher and a passion, and Hesse listens to trees. Watts says we come out of the world like leaves from a tree, and Oliver and O'Donohue ask what we owe the beautiful earth, which is also within us.
Solitude|The ancient sages choose to stand apart, and the Chinese and Japanese hermits find peace alone. John of the Cross meets God in solitude, and Browne delights in his own company. The moderns draw a line: Jung places loneliness in not being understood, Arendt separates solitude from loneliness, Hammarskjöld finds friendship cures it, and Paz calls it the deepest fact of being human.
Friendship|Aristotle and Cicero make the friend a second self and friendship the greatest good after virtue; Seneca adds trust, and Abelard loves the friend for himself, not his gifts. Later voices deepen it: Emerson's total trust, Hesse's recognition across difference, Lewis's circle in which each friend brings out another, Weil's virtue to be practised, Ali's lesson no school can teach.
Fate|Virgil says fortune favours the bold; Epictetus warns against tying a life to it. Montaigne and Pascal see chance everywhere, while Schiller sees destiny behind every accident. Novalis and Nietzsche turn fate into something we shape or love; Jung says what we leave unconscious returns as fate, Lispector that we are fated yet free, and Kundera listens to chance itself.
Change|Everything flows, says Heraclitus, and Zhuangzi and Ovid agree: forms change, nothing perishes. Han Shan watches the seasons turn; Sa'di sees life as one breath between two nothings. Coleridge longs for one constant, Emerson trusts only transition, Bergson makes change the way we grow, Huxley mocks consistency, and Mandela finds himself changed where the place has not.
Silence|Silence is first a stillness that sets the world right, then a lesson from the gods and the mystics' language of love; Ikkyū's silent flower opens. Tagore hears God's silence ripen our thoughts. Wittgenstein draws the line of what can be said, Huxley finds music nearest to silence, Hammarskjöld guards it amid noise, and Le Guin reminds us that every word needs silence before and after.
Beauty|Lao Tzu notes that naming beauty creates ugliness; Ovid that beauty fades. Marcus says the beautiful needs no praise, and Jami looks past finite beauty to the absolute. Hume and Kant make beauty a matter of feeling, then of a judgement all should share. Stendhal calls it a promise of happiness, Keats a joy for ever, Gibran eternity gazing at itself, and Morrison something one can do.
Hope|Boethius, like the Stoics, distrusts hope as a passion that clouds the mind; Kabir wants it fulfilled in this life. Burton and Hazlitt praise it as the best possession, and Dickinson gives it feathers. Unamuno finds it springing from despair, Tolkien sees it born when all is forlorn, and Solnit defends it as refusing to be certain the future is closed.
Justice|Plato calls doing injustice the greatest of evils; Mencius calls righteousness man's proper path, and the Qur'an enjoins justice and kindness. La Rochefoucauld and Pascal suspect justice serves fear and power. Emerson ties it to truth, Dostoevsky demands it of the universe, Solzhenitsyn finds it in having no part in injustice, King binds us in one garment of destiny, and Brand reminds us we are born into history.
Home|For the mystics home is where the soul came from: Boethius's lost land, Rumi's native nest, Kabir's true union. Wordsworth thinks we arrive from elsewhere; Hölderlin longs to return. Kierkegaard dissects homesickness, Frost makes home the place that has to take you in, and Rilke longs to dwell in passing things. Naipaul and Dillard weigh departure, and the planet as home or exile.
Knowing|The oldest teachers begin with the limit: know what you do not know. Socrates is wise because he knows he does not know, and Boethius thinks truth's sparks are already within. Saadi praises asking, Leonardo every kind of learning. Whitman and Proust say wisdom cannot be handed over, Jaspers that we are more than all we know, and Feynman that progress needs room for doubt.
